Translating a home improvement back catalog looks like a subtitle job until the first measurement. A viewer in Germany sees a 2x4 and a 16-penny nail; a viewer in the United States sees a 50x100 mm timber and a 3.1 mm nail. If the voice says the original number and the overlay shows the converted one, the result is not a translation. It is a contradiction.

DIY video translation means localizing instructional video with your own tools and review process instead of handing the catalog to an agency. For a maker or home improvement channel, the work carries a correctness requirement that entertainment localization does not. A mistranslated joke fails to land. A mistranslated wire gauge, torque value, or gas fitting can burn a house or break a wrist.

Advice about subtitles and dubbing usually stops at fluency. Instructional video also needs unit conversion, market-specific tool names, voltage and code differences, safety warnings that survive translation, and review by someone who has done the task in the target market.

## What makes DIY video translation a correctness problem

Failures cluster in four places:

- Numbers without units, or units that change meaning when converted.
- Tool names that exist in both markets but describe different objects.
- Steps that depend on a standard, voltage, or code that differs by country.
- Safety warnings that lose prominence after translation.

A 10-inch miter saw blade is not a 10-inch blade in countries that sell 250 mm and 260 mm blades. A toggle bolt may be sold as a spring toggle and need a different hole size. A translation can be grammatically perfect and still produce a wrong hole, a loose joint, or a tripped breaker.

### The three surfaces

Every instructional video has at least three translation surfaces:

1. The spoken track: procedure, caution, explanation.
2. The subtitle track: the same words in reading form.
3. The on-screen text: quantities, temperatures, cut lists, part numbers, warnings.

A fourth surface is the demonstration: hand position, the tool, the material on the bench. A [video translation](/features/video-translation) workflow can replace the voice and translate subtitles, but it cannot change the fact that the object on screen is an imperial drill bit. That gap is where review and local notes matter.

### Classify each video before translating

- Watch-only: technique, design, motivation. Subtitle translation is often enough.
- Follow-along: measurements, cut lists, assembly. Needs converted overlays and reviewed terminology.
- High-risk: electrical, gas, structural, or any sharp tool under load. Needs a market-qualified reviewer and explicit standards warnings.

Classification prevents two expensive mistakes: over-localizing a simple vlog and under-localizing a video where a wrong number matters.

## Measurements: imperial and metric conversion decisions

Three approaches are defensible, and the right one depends on what the number controls.

- Convert in place when the measurement is a target the viewer can hit with local tools. A 3/4-inch hole becomes 19 mm or 20 mm, depending on the nearest available bit.
- Dual-label when the dimension identifies a stock material. A 2x4 is nominal, not a true 2 by 4. Show "2x4 (50x100 mm nominal)" and let the viewer buy the local equivalent.
- Keep the original with a warning when the number is tied to a proprietary part, a thread standard, or a code requirement. Changing it is worse than leaving it.

Silent conversion is the worst option. If the voice says "three-quarters of an inch" and the subtitle says "19 mm," the viewer who listens and reads gets two instructions. Pick one authority per number.

### Convert in voice, overlay, or both

Convert in the voice when the viewer needs the number while their hands are busy. Convert in the overlay when the number is a reference the viewer will pause and read. Dual-label when the original number identifies the material or tool.

For a cut list, the overlay is the primary document. The voice can say "cut to the length shown on screen" instead of reading every number, which removes any chance of the spoken and written numbers diverging. In [subtitle translation](/features/subtitle-translation), keep the unit symbol attached to the number and avoid a line break between them, so "120 °C" never becomes "120" on one line and "°C" on the next.

### Rounding, tolerances, and nominal sizes

Conversion is not division. It is a decision about tolerance. A 1-inch dowel is 25.4 mm, but the dowel sold in a metric market may be 25 mm. A 1/2-inch pipe is not 12.7 mm pipe; nominal pipe sizes use a different naming system.

- For joinery, round to the nearest available tool size and state the tolerance.
- For plumbing and electrical, do not convert nominal trade sizes. Use the local standard name.
- For torque, convert and round to a value the viewer can set on a local wrench.
- For temperatures, convert exactly and note the oven or kiln setting.

### Worked example: a shelf bracket

The original reads: "Drill a 3/16-inch pilot hole, then drive the included 2-inch screws." The literal metric version, "Bohren Sie ein 5-mm-Pilotloch, dann verwenden Sie die mitgelieferten 50-mm-Schrauben," assumes the substitute screw matches the original. It may not: #8 wood screws have a head and thread a metric market does not stock, so a 50 mm replacement may not suit the pilot hole. A safer translation: "Use the included screws or a 4.5 mm wood screw of the same length; drill a 4 mm pilot hole in softwood, 4.5 mm in hardwood." The number changed, and so did the instruction.

## Tool, fastener, and material names across markets

The United States and the United Kingdom share a language and not a hardware store. A spanner is a wrench. A torch in the UK can be a flashlight. Drywall becomes plasterboard. A wire nut becomes a connector block. Particle board and chipboard are not always the same product. A translation that leaves these terms unchanged sounds fluent and sends the viewer to the wrong aisle.

### Fasteners and thread standards

The same fastener name can hide a different standard. Machine screws may be Unified National Coarse, Unified National Fine, metric coarse, or metric fine. A #10-32 screw is not interchangeable with an M5 even when the diameters are close. Pipe threads are worse: NPT, BSPT, and BSPP look similar and seal differently.

- Name the standard, not just the size.
- Show the thread pitch if the viewer must match an existing hole.
- Note when the local equivalent needs a different pilot hole or driver bit.
- Never substitute a fastener in a load-bearing or safety-critical application without an engineering check.

### Materials and finishes

Pressure-treated lumber has different chemical treatments and handling instructions by country. Galvanized can mean hot-dip or electroplated, which changes corrosion performance. Paint sheens differ, and VOC regulations change what is available. When the material is part of the safety story, such as treated wood dust or solvent-based finishes, localize the warning with the material: keep the hazard, the exposure route, and the protective action.

### Build a glossary before the batch

A glossary is a release artifact. Include the source term and market-specific target term, whether the term is spoken or shown, the approved substitute, and the source for the decision, such as a local supplier catalog or a code reference. It prevents the same tool from being translated three ways across a ten-video series. If you use an API workflow, the glossary can be part of the process described in the [documentation](/docs).

## Standards, voltages, and safety warnings after translation

A 120 V, 60 Hz tool does not run correctly on a 230 V, 50 Hz supply without a transformer, and some motors overheat on the wrong frequency. Wire colors, breaker types, residual current devices, and grounding rules differ. "Connect the black wire to the brass screw" is meaningless or dangerous in a market with different color codes.

### Electrical video requirements

- State the market and supply voltage at the start.
- Replace color-based instructions with terminal-based instructions where possible.
- Add a warning that local code and a licensed electrician govern the work.
- Remove or flag any step that assumes a particular breaker panel or outlet type.

### Gas, plumbing, and combustion

Gas work is the clearest case for market gating. Fitting types, pipe sizes, ventilation requirements, and leak-test procedures differ by country and sometimes by region. A translated gas tutorial gives false confidence and can be worse than no tutorial. If the video cannot be reviewed by a qualified person in the target market, restrict it with a clear warning or do not localize the procedure. Plumbing carries the same problem with nominal sizes, thread standards, and pressure ratings.

### Code, permits, and load assumptions

Structural work depends on snow loads, seismic zones, wind loads, and local permits. A beam size that works in one region may fail in another. The translation cannot simply convert dimensions; it has to state the assumption and tell the viewer to verify with a local professional. For high-risk videos, add a market warning card at the beginning and in the description. That is not legal boilerplate. It is a correctness feature.

### Keep safety warnings prominent

Viewers learn where warnings live. If the source video puts safety notes in the lower third with a yellow icon, the localized version should do the same; moving the position or dropping the icon makes the warning less visible even when the words are right.

Translate the hazard, not the sentence. "Be careful with the blade" is weaker than "Keep hands clear of the blade; unplug the saw before changing blades." A warning should name the hazard, the consequence, and the preventive action. A language-only reviewer can confirm grammar and miss the danger entirely.

## Demonstrations, voice timing, and text overlays

Instructional voice-over often describes what is happening now: "Applying steady pressure as the bit enters the wood." The translated voice has to fit the same action window. If the target sentence is longer, the voice may still be talking about the first cut when the video shows the second, and the viewer hears a description that is no longer true.

- Rewrite the narration to describe the action in fewer words.
- Move detail into the subtitle or the overlay.
- Split the line and let the voice pause while the action continues.
- Use [audio translation](/features/audio-translation) to replace the spoken track while preserving the timing of the original performance.

### Timing budgets and reading speed

Subtitles have a reading budget. A line on screen for two seconds carries fewer words than one on screen for four. When translation expands the text, either the subtitle stays longer or the viewer misses the end. In a demonstration, the subtitle cannot outlast the action without overlapping the next step.

Set a character limit per second, then check the result against the video. If the translated line exceeds the limit, shorten the line, not the display time. The [subtitle generation](/features/subtitle-generation) step should produce a timed file you can inspect for overflow before you record or synthesize the voice.

### Instructions that cannot be shortened

Some instructions lose their meaning if shortened. "Do not stand on the top two rungs of the ladder" carries a specific condition. If the target language needs more words, extend the shot or add a brief pause. If neither is possible, move the condition to an overlay that stays on screen.

### Overlay inventory: quantities, temperatures, cut lists

Before translation, list every text element with its timecode, content, and unit: quantities and dimensions, temperatures and speeds, cut lists and shopping lists, part numbers, safety warnings and icons, labels for tools and materials.

Keep the unit symbol with the number, and use the local decimal separator where the market expects it. In many European markets a comma separates decimals and a period separates thousands; a mixed convention in one video looks careless and can be misread.

A cut list is a data table, not prose. Translate the column headers, the units, and the material names, but keep the numbers aligned with their labels, so that "2x4 @ 24 in" becomes "50x100 mm @ 610 mm" and the viewer can cut without converting in their head. For [subtitle-to-audio](/features/subtitle-to-audio) workflows, check that the synthesized voice pronounces unit abbreviations correctly; "mm" may be read as letters, and "°C" may be skipped.

## Review passes: who checks a DIY video translation

The best reviewer has done the task in the target market. They know which substitute is sold, which code applies, and which warning is taken seriously. A language-only reviewer catches grammar and tone but not a wrong pilot hole or a missing ground connection. For high-risk videos, use both.

### A review checklist

- Every measurement has a unit and a local equivalent.
- Every tool and material name matches the local catalog.
- Every standard, voltage, and code reference is correct for the market.
- Every safety warning names the hazard, consequence, and action.
- Every overlay fits and matches the voice.
- Every step is possible with locally available parts.

The reviewer marks each item pass, fail, or not applicable. A fail requires a script change, not a comment.

### Two-pass review for high-risk videos

High-risk videos get two passes. The first checks the script against the glossary and the standards. The second watches the localized video with sound and subtitles on, looking for contradictions between what is said, what is shown, and what is written. That pass catches the most dangerous error: a correct voice and a correct overlay that disagree.

## Batch-localizing a back catalog in priority order

Score each video on two axes: risk of harm if the instruction is wrong, and likely value in the target market. High risk and high value goes first with full review; low risk and low value can wait or receive subtitles only.

### Release in waves

1. Translate and publish the three to five highest-value, lowest-risk videos to test the glossary and the review process.
2. Add high-risk videos after a task-qualified reviewer is in place.
3. Fill in the long tail with subtitle-only localization.
4. Revisit the first wave with corrections from viewers and reviewers.

Waves limit the blast radius of a terminology mistake and give you watch-time data before you dub every video.

### Asset reuse and measurement

Name source and localized assets clearly: project, market, language, version, date. Store the glossary, overlay inventory, and review checklist with the project, so a correction points you to every video using the same term. Keep original and localized audio separate so a future fix does not require rebuilding from scratch.

Views alone do not tell you whether the translation is correct. Track retention at measurement-heavy sections, comments reporting a wrong step, and corrections per thousand views. A spike in questions at the same timestamp usually means the overlay is unclear.

## Viewer comments and corrections in languages you do not speak

Corrections arrive in many languages and with varying reliability:

- A correction naming a standard, a local product, or a code section is high priority.
- "This is wrong" without a reason is low priority until confirmed.
- A correction repeated across multiple viewers is likely real.
- A correction that changes a safety step gets reviewed before you act.

Machine translation can help you read the comment. It should not be the final judgment on a technical correction.

### Pinned corrections and description updates

When a correction is confirmed, update the description and pin a comment in the target language. If the error is in the video, add a correction card at the timestamp. Do not silently re-upload; viewers who watched the old version will not know. For a significant correction, consider a short follow-up video.

### Closing the loop without losing authority

Thank the viewer in their language if you can, and state what you changed. A creator who corrects a wrong measurement gains credibility; one who argues with a qualified viewer loses it. For a language you do not speak, ask a bilingual community member or reviewer to draft the response, keeping it factual: what was wrong, what is correct, what the viewer should do.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to convert every measurement in a DIY video?

No. Convert measurements that control a cut, a hole, a torque, or a safety condition. Keep nominal trade sizes and proprietary part numbers in their original standard, with a local equivalent where one exists.

### Should the voice and the subtitle use the same units?

Yes, unless the overlay is the single authority and the voice refers to it. If the voice says one number and the subtitle says another, the viewer must choose, and that choice is the beginning of a wrong cut.

### How do I handle tool names that differ between two countries that share a language?

Use the local term from a supplier catalog, not a dictionary. Build a glossary with the source term, the market term, and the approved substitute. If the tool is unavailable, name the local equivalent and note any difference in use.

### Can I localize electrical videos myself?

You can translate the language, but do not publish electrical instructions for a market unless a qualified person has reviewed the standards, voltage, and code. Without that review, add a market warning and point viewers to a licensed electrician.

### What is the biggest timing risk in dubbed instructional video?

The voice describing an action that has already moved on. Translation often expands the sentence, so the narration lags behind the hands. Rewrite for length, move detail to the overlay, or extend the shot before accepting a sync problem.

### How should I prioritize a large back catalog?

Score each video by risk of harm and likely value in the market. Localize high-value, low-risk videos first to test your glossary and review process, add high-risk videos only after a task-qualified reviewer is in place, and fill the long tail with subtitle-only work.

### Where can I ask about a specific localization workflow?

If you need help scoping a batch, [contact](/contact) the team with the languages, the video count, and the risk level of the content. Bring your glossary and overlay inventory; they are the fastest way to an accurate answer.

## Conclusion

Start with one video, not the whole catalog. Pick a follow-along project with measurements and at least one safety note, then run it through the full process: glossary, converted overlays, a reviewed script, and a watch-through with sound and subtitles on. The goal is not a perfect translation but a repeatable process that catches the errors which change a result.

Then decide what to do with the rest. If the first video shows your glossary is thin or your review path unclear, fix that before scaling. If the process holds, move to a wave of similar videos and keep electrical, gas, and structural content behind a qualified review. The catalog can wait. A wrong instruction cannot be unpublished from a viewer's workbench.
