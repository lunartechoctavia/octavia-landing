## A Language That Is Not French

Haitian Creole is spoken by roughly twelve million people — essentially the entire population of Haiti — plus substantial diaspora communities in the United States, Canada, the Dominican Republic, the Bahamas, and France. In the US, concentrations in Florida, New York, and Massachusetts are large enough that public agencies in those states routinely carry language access obligations for it.

The first and most consequential thing to get right is that Haitian Creole is a distinct language, not a dialect or informal variety of French.

The confusion is understandable — the majority of its vocabulary derives from French — and it is nonetheless a substantive error with real consequences. Haitian Creole has its own grammar, which differs fundamentally from French: no grammatical gender, no verb conjugation by person, a distinct system of tense and aspect markers, and a different word order in several constructions. Its orthography is phonemic and standardised, and looks quite different from French spelling.

Critically, most Haitian Creole speakers do not speak French. French is spoken fluently by a small educated minority; Creole is spoken by everyone. Serving French content to a Haitian audience reaches a fraction of it, and the choice is noticed — the language question in Haiti carries a long history around class and access.

For anyone providing language access, the practical rule is simple: if the audience is Haitian, the language is Haitian Creole unless you have specific reason to believe otherwise.

## Standard Orthography

Haitian Creole has an official standardised orthography, established in 1979 and used in education, government, and publishing. It is phonemic — spelling maps consistently to pronunciation — which makes it far more regular than French.

Practical points:

**Use the standard orthography.** Older French-influenced spellings still circulate, particularly in materials produced by non-Haitian organisations or by older diaspora writers, and they are inconsistent and non-standard. Content should follow the official system.

**Do not French-ify the spelling.** Systems trained heavily on French sometimes produce Creole with French-style spellings. This is a recognisable error and it undermines credibility with the audience.

**Diacritics matter.** The orthography uses a grave accent on certain vowels to distinguish sounds, and these must render correctly. Verify on every output surface, including platform metadata fields, which frequently strip them.

**Apostrophes and elision.** Creole uses contracted forms extensively, and the conventions are standardised. Inconsistent handling is a common quality marker.

**Text expansion** relative to English is modest, typically ten to twenty percent, and Creole often runs shorter than French for equivalent content because it lacks French's inflectional endings.

## Literacy and Why Audio Comes First

This is the single most important practical consideration for Haitian Creole video localization, and it is where the language differs sharply from most European targets.

Literacy rates in Haiti are low relative to global averages, and — importantly — many people who do read have been schooled in French rather than Creole, which means they may read French more comfortably than the Creole they actually speak. The standardised Creole orthography is relatively recent, and older speakers may never have been taught it.

The implications for video are direct:

**Dubbed audio reaches substantially more people than Creole subtitles.** This is not a preference or a quality judgement; it is a reach question. A translated Creole subtitle track serves a smaller audience than translated Creole narration.

**Audio-only versions have real value.** Radio remains an important medium in Haiti, and audio extracted from localized video serves that channel directly. Generating audio from a reviewed transcript makes this nearly free.

**Do not rely on text for critical information.** Emergency instructions, health guidance, and eligibility information delivered only as on-screen text will not reach a substantial part of the audience.

**Subtitles still have a place**, particularly for diaspora audiences with higher literacy and for viewers watching in sound-off contexts, but they should supplement rather than replace audio.

For organisations whose language access programmes were built around translated documents, Haitian Creole is often the case that makes the limitation of that approach visible.

## Where Haitian Creole Content Matters Most

The demand for Haitian Creole localization is concentrated in specific content types.

**Public health and medical information.** Patient instructions, medication guidance, vaccination information, maternal and child health, and outbreak communication. Comprehension here has direct health consequences.

**Emergency and disaster communication.** Haiti faces recurring hurricanes and seismic risk, and diaspora communities in Florida face hurricane exposure. Pre-translated emergency templates in Creole are a genuine preparedness measure.

**Immigration, legal rights, and benefits.** Diaspora communities need eligibility, procedure, and rights information, and errors here cost people entitlements.

**Education and school communication.** US districts with Haitian communities carry family communication obligations.

**Financial services and remittances.** Remittances are economically central to Haiti, and the communications around them are frequently only in English or French.

**Agricultural and development extension content.** Practical instruction for rural audiences, which is exactly the audience least served by text.

**Religious and community content**, which circulates widely and is a significant part of community media.

This concentration in public-interest content shapes the quality requirements: much of it is consequential, and much of it is produced by organisations without Creole speakers on staff.

## Terminology and Review

Haitian Creole vocabulary presents some specific challenges for automated translation.

**Technical and medical vocabulary** is an area where Creole is actively developing. Terms exist but usage varies, and some technical concepts are commonly expressed through French or English borrowings in practice. Lock terminology explicitly rather than letting each pass choose.

**Institutional names** — agencies, programmes, forms, benefit types — should generally be retained in their official form so the audience can recognise them on documents and signage, with a Creole explanation alongside.

**Avoid over-borrowing from French.** Where a genuine Creole word exists and is in use, it should be preferred. Systems that lean on French vocabulary produce output that is comprehensible to the educated minority and less accessible to everyone else, which defeats the purpose.

**Register should be direct and plain.** Creole public communication works best when it is concrete and instructional rather than institutional.

**Native review is essential**, and specifically review by someone from the target community rather than a French speaker working from proximity. This is the most common shortcut taken with this language and it produces poor results.

## Voice and Delivery

Practical points for generated audio:

**Pronunciation of retained French and English terms** should follow Creole phonology rather than the source language, which is how speakers actually say them.

**Pace should be unhurried.** Much of this content is consequential instruction delivered to audiences who may be under stress. Natural, deliberate pacing rather than compression to match source duration matters more here than in most contexts.

**A warm, direct register works better** than authoritative institutional delivery, particularly for health and community content where trust is the operative factor.

**Diaspora versus Haiti audiences** differ somewhat in vocabulary exposure, with diaspora speech incorporating more English. For content aimed specifically at one, adjust accordingly.

## Distribution

Reaching this audience effectively requires attention to channel.

**Mobile-first with data constraints**, particularly in Haiti where connectivity is expensive and intermittent. Keep file sizes modest and provide downloadable versions where possible.

**Radio remains significant** in Haiti, which makes audio deliverables genuinely useful rather than an afterthought.

**Messaging platforms** carry a large share of content sharing within diaspora communities, and content formatted for easy sharing performs better than content requiring a website visit.

**Community and faith organisations** are trusted intermediaries and are often far more effective distribution partners than official channels.

**Diaspora radio and community media** in Florida, New York, and Massachusetts reach concentrated audiences directly.

## A Working Checklist

- Confirm the target is Haitian Creole, not French. Do not assume French serves a Haitian audience.
- Use the official standardised orthography; reject French-influenced spellings.
- Verify grave-accent diacritics render on every output surface, including platform metadata.
- Prioritise dubbed audio over subtitle-only delivery, given literacy realities.
- Produce an audio-only version for radio and messaging distribution.
- Never deliver critical information as on-screen text alone.
- Lock institutional names in their official form with Creole explanation alongside.
- Prefer genuine Creole vocabulary over French borrowings where a Creole term is in use.
- Keep register plain, concrete, and instructional.
- Have output reviewed by a native Creole speaker from the target community, not a French speaker.

## Planning a First Project

A first localization project into Haitian Creole goes more smoothly with a few decisions made deliberately rather than discovered mid-way.

**Pick content that suits the language, not just the market.** Evergreen explanatory content with clean single-speaker audio is the right starting point. Content dense with overlapping speech, heavy idiom, or tight narration-to-visual sync will expose every weakness in the workflow at once and teach you less.

**Budget review time honestly.** Expect the first assets in a new language to need substantially more review than the tenth, because the recurring corrections have not yet become locked terminology. Pricing or scheduling on first-job effort will overstate your steady-state cost considerably.

**Recruit the reviewer before you need them.** A native speaker who knows the subject matter is worth considerably more than a general translator, and finding one under deadline pressure produces a worse choice than finding one in advance.

**Run a short sample end to end first.** Two minutes of representative content through the full pipeline — transcript, terminology, translation, generation, delivery — surfaces rendering, timing, and register problems while they are cheap to fix.

**Keep the reviewed transcript.** It is the input to every future language and every future subtitle track, and regenerating it each time discards correction work already paid for.

**Plan for audio as the primary deliverable.** Given literacy patterns, a subtitle-only approach reaches a meaningfully smaller audience, and an audio-only version for radio and messaging distribution costs almost nothing extra.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Haitian Creole a dialect of French?**

No. It is a distinct language with its own grammar — no grammatical gender, no person-based verb conjugation, a separate tense and aspect system — and its own standardised phonemic orthography. Most of its vocabulary derives from French, which causes the confusion, but most Haitian Creole speakers do not speak French. Serving French content to a Haitian audience reaches only a small educated minority.

**Should I provide subtitles or dubbed audio?**

Dubbed audio, as the priority. Literacy rates are low relative to global averages, and many people who read were schooled in French rather than Creole, so they may read French more comfortably than the Creole they speak. Creole subtitles reach a meaningfully smaller audience than Creole narration. Subtitles are a useful supplement, not a substitute.

**Which spelling system should be used?**

The official standardised orthography established in 1979, which is phonemic and used in Haitian education, government, and publishing. Older French-influenced spellings still circulate, particularly in materials produced by non-Haitian organisations, and they are inconsistent and non-standard. Systems trained heavily on French sometimes produce French-style spellings, which the audience recognises as an error.

**How should institutional and programme names be handled?**

Retain them in their official form so the audience can match them to documents, forms, and signage, and add a plain Creole explanation alongside. Translating a benefit programme's name leaves the viewer unable to find the thing you described, which is the same failure that occurs in any public-sector localization.

**Who should review Haitian Creole output?**

A native Creole speaker from the target community — Haiti or the specific diaspora population you are addressing. The common shortcut is to have a French speaker review on the assumption of proximity, and it produces poor results because the languages differ grammatically and because French-influenced output is precisely the failure mode you are trying to catch.

**Can we use the same content for Haiti and for diaspora audiences?**

Largely yes, with awareness that diaspora speech incorporates more English and that diaspora literacy in written Creole is often higher. Where content references local institutions, procedures, or services, those differ entirely between Haiti and a US state, and that is a content difference rather than a language one — the explanation needs rewriting, not just retranslating.

**Is machine translation reliable for Haitian Creole?**

Less so than for major European languages, because the available training data is comparatively limited. Expect a higher correction rate and budget accordingly, keep sentences short and concrete in the source, and treat native review by someone from the target community as mandatory rather than optional for anything consequential.
