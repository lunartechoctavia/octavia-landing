## Why Extension Video Is Different

Agricultural extension — the practice of getting research-based knowledge to farmers — has traditionally depended on people. An extension officer visits, demonstrates, answers questions, and returns. It works well and it does not scale: officer-to-farmer ratios in many countries run into the thousands, and remote smallholders may see an officer rarely or never.

Video has become the main scaling mechanism, and it works genuinely well for this purpose because agriculture is a demonstrative skill. Showing correct spacing, showing how to identify a pest, showing a grafting technique — these transfer visually in a way that written instruction never has.

But extension video carries requirements that most video localization advice does not address. The audience frequently speaks a regional or local language rather than a national one. Literacy varies widely. Devices are shared and modest. Connectivity is expensive and intermittent. And the content is time-critical in a way few other categories are: advice about planting is useless after planting season has passed.

Getting the localization right for this audience is less about linguistic polish and more about reach, timing, and trust.

## Language Selection Is the Whole Game

The most common failure in agricultural extension content is producing it in the national language when the farming population speaks something else.

National languages are the languages of government, education, and urban life. In many countries, rural smallholder populations — particularly women farmers, older farmers, and those with limited formal schooling — operate primarily in a regional or local language and follow the national language imperfectly at best.

Content in the national language reaches extension officers, agricultural graduates, and larger commercial farmers. It reaches the smallholder majority far less well, and that majority is usually the intended beneficiary.

Practical guidance:

**Select languages from agricultural census and extension records**, not from national statistics. The question is what farmers in your target districts speak, not what the country speaks.

**Prioritise by production area, not population.** A language spoken by fewer people who farm your target crop matters more than a larger language whose speakers do not.

**Expect to serve languages with limited digital resources.** Many agriculturally important languages have modest written traditions and limited support in translation systems. Output quality will be lower and native review correspondingly more important.

**Women farmers may have different language profiles** than men in the same community, with lower national-language proficiency in many contexts. Content aimed at women farmers should reflect that.

**Verify with local partners rather than assuming.** Extension services, cooperatives, and farmer organisations know precisely what their members speak.

## Audio Comes First

The literacy realities in this audience make audio the primary channel and text a supplement.

Practical consequences:

**Dubbed narration reaches substantially more farmers than subtitles.** This is a reach question rather than a preference.

**Audio-only versions have independent value.** Rural radio remains a major agricultural information channel in many countries, and audio extracted from a localized video serves it directly. Generating audio from a reviewed transcript makes this nearly free.

**Never put critical information only in on-screen text.** Application rates, dilution ratios, safety intervals, and timing instructions must be spoken.

**Numbers deserve special care.** Application rates and dilution ratios are the content most likely to cause harm if misunderstood, and they should be spoken clearly, repeated, and shown visually as well.

**Consider visual-only versions.** For some content, a well-shot demonstration with minimal narration transfers across languages with almost no localization cost, which is worth exploiting for techniques that are genuinely visual.

## Terminology and Local Knowledge

Agricultural terminology presents a specific challenge: the technically correct term and the term farmers actually use are frequently different.

Categories requiring careful handling:

**Crop varieties.** Local names for varieties often differ from official or scientific designations, and farmers know them by local name. Using only the official name means farmers cannot identify what is being discussed.

**Pests and diseases.** Local names are usually descriptive and widely known. Scientific names are precise and unfamiliar. Content should generally lead with the local name and include the scientific name for cross-reference.

**Units and measures.** Traditional measures — a specific container, a defined plot, a bundle — are frequently more meaningful to farmers than metric units. Where a product must be applied at a metric rate, translating that into a locally meaningful measure is a genuine service and reduces application errors.

**Agrochemical names.** Product brand names, active ingredient names, and safety terminology all need locked handling, matched to what appears on the actual container label the farmer will buy.

**Soil, weather, and seasonal vocabulary.** Local agricultural calendars and terms for seasonal periods are precise and meaningful, and mapping content to them is more useful than calendar months.

The agrochemical labelling point matters for safety. If the video uses different terminology from the product container, the farmer cannot connect the instruction to the product, and misapplication follows.

## Seasonal Timing

Extension content is time-critical in a way that most video is not.

Advice about land preparation is useful before land preparation and worthless after. Pest identification content matters when the pest is present. Harvest and post-harvest handling advice needs to arrive before harvest.

Operational implications:

**Plan localization against the agricultural calendar**, working backwards from when farmers need the information.

**Localize ahead of season, not during.** Content produced during the relevant window arrives too late, since distribution to rural audiences takes time.

**Different agro-ecological zones have different calendars.** A single country may have several planting seasons, and content timed for one zone arrives wrongly for another.

**Build an evergreen core and a seasonal layer.** Techniques and principles are stable; timing-specific advice is not.

**Batch seasonal content by language ahead of the window**, which is exactly the workload profile that batch processing suits.

## Trust and Distribution

Agricultural advice is acted on only if it is believed, and farmers are appropriately sceptical of advice from unfamiliar sources — particularly advice that involves spending money on inputs.

What builds credibility:

**Local voices.** Content narrated by someone who sounds local, or featuring farmers from the same region, is trusted more than content narrated in a distant urban register. This is a genuine argument for careful voice selection rather than a default.

**Farmer testimony.** Content showing a farmer from a comparable context describing their own results is more persuasive than expert instruction.

**Distribution through trusted intermediaries.** Extension officers, cooperatives, farmer field schools, agro-dealers, and community organisations carry trust that a direct digital channel does not.

**Group viewing.** Extension video is frequently watched in groups, facilitated by an extension officer or lead farmer, with discussion afterwards. This format is markedly more effective than individual viewing, and it means content should be designed to prompt discussion rather than only to inform.

**Honesty about limitations.** Content that acknowledges when a technique will not suit every context is more credible than blanket recommendation.

The group viewing point has a direct design consequence: content intended for facilitated viewing benefits from natural pause points and from an accompanying facilitator guide in the same language.

## Delivery Under Constraint

**Data is expensive and connectivity intermittent.** Keep file sizes small, provide low-bandwidth versions, and make content downloadable for offline viewing and sharing.

**Devices are modest and shared.** A household may have one smartphone. Content that requires a recent device or a large download excludes people.

**Messaging platforms carry sharing.** Content sized and formatted for messaging distribution spreads through farmer networks in a way website-hosted content does not.

**Physical distribution still matters.** Extension officers with content preloaded on tablets or memory cards reach farmers that digital distribution does not.

**Radio remains significant**, which is why the audio-only deliverable earns its place.

**Screen conditions are difficult.** Outdoor viewing in bright light means high-contrast visuals and captions if used.

## A Working Checklist

- Select languages from agricultural census and extension records, not national statistics.
- Prioritise by production area and target crop rather than raw speaker numbers.
- Check whether women farmers in the target area have a different language profile.
- Prioritise dubbed audio; treat text as a supplement.
- Produce an audio-only version for rural radio distribution.
- Lead with local names for crops, varieties, pests, and diseases; include scientific names for reference.
- Express application rates in locally meaningful measures alongside metric units.
- Match agrochemical terminology to the product container label.
- Plan localization against the agricultural calendar, working backwards from the need date.
- Design for facilitated group viewing with natural pause points and a facilitator guide.

## Working With Extension Networks

Extension officers, lead farmers, and farmer organisations are the distribution and credibility layer, and content designed without them underperforms regardless of quality.

**Involve them in content selection.** They know which questions farmers actually ask, which is frequently not what a research programme assumes.

**Give them the content on their own devices.** Officers with material preloaded reach farmers that digital distribution does not, particularly in low-connectivity areas.

**Design for facilitation.** Content intended to be shown to a group and discussed should have natural pause points and an accompanying facilitator note in the same language.

**Train facilitators on the material.** An officer who has watched the content and understands its limits facilitates far better than one seeing it for the first time alongside the group.

**Collect feedback systematically.** Officers hear immediately what confused people, what farmers rejected, and what they asked afterwards. This is the highest-quality content feedback available and it usually goes unrecorded.

**Credit and involve local partners.** Content produced with visible involvement from a trusted local organisation is acted on more readily than content that appears to arrive from outside.

Programmes that treat extension networks as co-producers rather than distribution channels consistently see higher uptake of the practices the content promotes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should extension content be in the national language or local languages?**

Local languages, for smallholder audiences. National-language content reaches extension officers, agricultural graduates, and larger commercial farmers reasonably well, and reaches the smallholder majority — particularly women, older farmers, and those with limited formal schooling — much less well. Select languages from agricultural census and extension records for your target districts rather than from national statistics.

**Is subtitling sufficient for farmer audiences?**

Generally no. Literacy varies widely and dubbed narration reaches substantially more farmers than subtitles. Critical information — application rates, dilution ratios, safety intervals, timing — must be spoken rather than shown only as on-screen text, and ideally repeated and shown visually as well.

**How should application rates and measurements be handled?**

Express them in locally meaningful measures alongside metric units. Traditional measures — a specific container, a defined plot area, a bundle — are frequently more actionable for farmers than metric quantities, and translating rates into them reduces misapplication. Match agrochemical product terminology to what appears on the actual container the farmer buys.

**Should we use local or scientific names for pests and crop varieties?**

Lead with the local name, which is what farmers actually use and recognise, and include the scientific or official name for cross-reference. Content using only official designations leaves farmers unable to identify what is being discussed, which defeats the purpose of the material.

**When should seasonal content be localized?**

Well ahead of the relevant window, working backwards from when farmers need the information, because distribution to rural audiences takes time and advice about planting is worthless after planting season. Note that a single country may have several agro-ecological zones with different calendars, so timing that suits one region may arrive wrongly for another.

**Should extension video feature farmers or experts?**

Both, in different roles. Technique demonstration benefits from expert precision, but adoption evidence is considerably more persuasive coming from a farmer in a comparable context describing their own results. Content that combines a clear demonstration with genuine local testimony consistently outperforms either alone, and the testimony is the part that must be locally recorded rather than translated.

**How long should an extension video be?**

Short, and focused on one practice. Three to five minutes covering a single technique is far more usable than a twenty-minute overview, because it fits fragmented viewing, is easier to share, and can be rewatched at the point of application. It also localizes more efficiently, since a change to one practice does not require reprocessing everything.
