## The Asymmetry Nobody Fixes

Most major clubs and leagues have known for a decade that the majority of their following lives outside their home country. Commercial departments build entire strategies on that fact — international tours, regional sponsorships, market-specific merchandising, localized social accounts.

Then the content operation produces everything in one language.

The consequence is a two-tier fanbase. Domestic supporters get the manager's press conference, the training-ground feature, the tactical breakdown, the academy documentary, the injury update. International supporters get the highlights, a translated caption, and whatever a fan account decides to summarise for them.

That gap is exactly where fan attachment either forms or does not. Highlights generate attention; the surrounding content generates the identification that turns attention into a shirt sale, a subscription, and a lifetime allegiance. A fan who only ever sees goals has a relationship with a highlight reel, not with a club.

Sports organisations are unusually well placed to fix this, because their content is high-volume, formulaic in structure, and produced against a repeating calendar. That is a workflow problem with a known shape, not a creative one.

## Sorting Content by Speed and Value

Sports content divides cleanly by how quickly it decays.

**Minutes-to-hours.** Live reaction, post-match interviews, goal clips, team news, injury announcements. Enormous engagement, almost none of it after the first day. Only subtitles are realistic here, and speed matters more than polish.

**Same-week.** Press conferences, tactical analysis, training features, match previews and reviews. Enough time for dubbed audio with a review pass. This is where the highest-value localization sits, because the content is substantial enough to build attachment and slow enough to handle properly.

**Evergreen.** Player profiles, academy features, club history, stadium tours, documentary series, legends content. No deadline, long shelf life, and by far the best return on full localization. A well-made player profile stays useful for as long as the player is at the club.

**Archive.** Classic matches, historical documentaries, past-season retrospectives. Huge volume, zero deadline, and typically monetised through subscription platforms where catalogue depth in a market is the product.

The instinct is to start with match-day content because that is where the engagement numbers are. The better return is starting with evergreen and archive, where the work is not wasted after twenty-four hours and where a subscription proposition in a new market actually becomes viable.

## Player Interviews Are the Hard Part

Interview content is the most valuable material a club produces for international fans and the most technically awkward to localize.

The specific problems:

**Multilingual squads.** A single dressing room may contain a dozen first languages. A post-match interview might be conducted in the club's language by a player speaking their third. Transcription accuracy on heavily accented, non-native speech is the single biggest quality variable, and it is worth testing before committing to a workflow.

**Mixed-language interviews.** Players routinely switch languages mid-answer, or answer in one language a question asked in another. Systems that assume one language per file will mangle these.

**Environment.** Pitchside and mixed-zone interviews are recorded in noise — crowd, PA, other interviews happening a metre away. Audio quality frequently sits below what reliable transcription needs.

**Speaker overlap.** Press conferences with several journalists talking over each other, and player interviews with multiple participants, require genuine speaker separation to be usable.

Practical mitigations that cost little: use lapel or handheld microphones rather than camera audio wherever the setting allows; record press conferences from the mult-box feed rather than from the room; load a locked vocabulary of every player, staff member, club, competition, and stadium name before processing anything; and verify the transcript and speaker labels before generating any audio.

That last point is where most of the quality is won. A misheard player name propagated into nine languages is a far more visible error than a slightly awkward sentence.

## Names, Terminology, and Getting It Right

Sports terminology is a minefield for automated translation precisely because so much of it looks like ordinary vocabulary.

What needs locking as terminology:

- Every player, coach, and staff name, with the correct form and any market-specific rendering. Names must never be translated, and systems left unconstrained will occasionally try.
- Club and competition names in the form used in each market, which is often not a translation. Competitions frequently have official localized names that differ from a literal rendering.
- Stadium and training ground names.
- Position names, which differ meaningfully across footballing cultures and are not reliably interchangeable.
- Tactical vocabulary, where the same concept has established but non-obvious equivalents per language.
- Officiating and rules terminology, which is defined language with official translations published by governing bodies.
- Club-specific culture — nickname, anthem references, supporter terminology, historical shorthand — which usually should be preserved rather than translated.
- Sponsor and commercial naming, which is contractually specified and must be exact.

Governing bodies publish official multilingual rules terminology. Using those renderings rather than generating fresh ones is both more accurate and easier to defend when a broadcaster or federation queries it.

## Commentary Versus Narration

A useful distinction that clubs often collapse.

**Commentary is a performance.** Live match commentary carries emotion, timing, escalation, and local idiom. It does not dub well, and audiences in most markets prefer a native commentator over a translated one. For live and highlights content, the sustainable answer is either local commentary talent or no commentary at all — a clean stadium-sound version that any market can use.

**Narration is information.** Tactical analysis, feature packages, documentaries, and explainers are scripted and informational. These localize well, and the audience's expectation is comprehension rather than performance.

The practical implication for production: deliver highlights and match content with commentary on a separate audio stem from stadium atmosphere. This lets each market choose — original commentary, local commentary, or clean atmosphere — without a re-edit. Clubs that bake commentary into the master have removed that option permanently.

Where a full separated stem is unavailable, dialogue separation can recover a usable atmosphere bed from a mixed track, which makes archive material workable that otherwise would not be.

## Voice and Identity

Sports has a particular dynamic around voice: fans know what their players sound like, and a synthesised version of a well-known athlete raises expectations and questions that a corporate narrator does not.

Sensible positions:

**Narration and feature content.** A consistent professional voice per language works well. Fans do not need the club's in-house narrator to sound identical everywhere.

**Player interviews.** This is where it gets sensitive. Cloning a player's voice to speak a language they do not speak is technically straightforward and reputationally delicate. It requires explicit consent, and image-rights arrangements for professional athletes are typically negotiated and complex — a club cannot assume its standard contract covers voice synthesis. Many organisations sensibly choose subtitles for player speech and dub only the narration around it.

**Manager and official communications.** Similar considerations, with the added factor that a manager's words carry contractual and disciplinary weight. A mistranslated comment about an official or an opponent creates real problems.

The conservative pattern that most organisations land on: dub narration, subtitle the humans. It preserves authenticity, avoids the consent complexity entirely, and matches what fans in most markets already expect from sports content.

## Rights and Territories

Sports media rights are territorial, exclusive, and litigated. Localization interacts with them in ways that are easy to get wrong.

Before localizing any content containing match footage, confirm:

- Whether the club or league holds the rights to distribute that footage in the target territory at all, and in what window.
- Whether a broadcast partner holds exclusivity that a club's own localized channel would breach.
- Whether licensed music in the content is cleared for those territories, which does not extend automatically.
- Whether player image rights permit the intended use in that market.
- Whether competition regulations restrict what clubs may publish and when.

The safest starting point for a club building international content is material it wholly owns and controls: training-ground footage, interviews it conducted, features it produced, academy and community content, and club history. That is a large library, it carries the fan-attachment value, and it avoids the rights complexity that match footage brings.

## Choosing Markets

Sports organisations have unusually good data for this and often ignore it.

Useful inputs: social following by country, merchandise shipping destinations, streaming and app usage by market, ticket purchases by international visitors, existing regional sponsorship commitments, the nationality of current and recent players, tour history, and the language of unofficial fan communities.

That last one is the most undervalued signal. Where fans have organised themselves into a language community and are informally translating club content, demand has been demonstrated conclusively. Those communities are also the best available reviewers and, handled respectfully, the best possible partners rather than competitors.

A common mistake is prioritising markets by population rather than by demonstrated affinity. A mid-sized market where the club has a genuine following and a player connection will outperform a large market with no existing relationship.

## Where to Start

Pick two markets with demonstrated affinity, not the two largest.

Localize the evergreen library first — player profiles, club history, stadium and training features, academy content. This is owned content with no rights complexity and no deadline pressure.

Subtitle the fast-turnaround content in the same two languages, which is cheap and immediately visible to fans.

Build the terminology asset properly during the first month, since every name, competition, and tactical term recurs constantly across everything else you will ever produce.

Measure per market: follower growth, watch time, completion rate, subscription conversion, and merchandise. Hold for at least two quarters before judging, since fan-base growth is slow.

Then expand, treating the second market rollout as a repeatable process rather than a new project.

## Working With Fan Communities

Most clubs with a genuine international following already have unofficial translation happening. Supporter groups subtitle interviews, translate press conferences, and summarise club news for their own language communities, usually within hours and usually for free.

The instinct in some organisations is to treat this as a rights problem. That is almost always the wrong response — these communities are the club's most engaged international supporters, and enforcement against them is both reputationally costly and strategically self-defeating.

The productive approach treats them as partners:

**Use their activity as market evidence.** Where fans have organised to translate content, demand has been proven more convincingly than any survey could. The languages with active fan translation should be the first languages the club serves officially.

**Recruit reviewers from these communities.** They know the club's culture, the supporter terminology, the historical references, and the register that fans expect. They are better reviewers than any external translator and they usually regard the invitation as recognition rather than work.

**Give them official content early** rather than leaving them to work from broadcast clips of uncertain provenance. This improves quality, resolves the rights ambiguity, and costs nothing.

**Credit them.** Attribution matters enormously to volunteer communities and costs the club nothing at all.

**Do not simply take over.** A club that launches official localized content and simultaneously shuts down the fan effort loses goodwill disproportionate to the gain. Official and community content can coexist, serving different needs.

Clubs that build this relationship well acquire a distributed, motivated, culturally fluent quality-assurance network that no vendor could supply.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should we dub player interviews or subtitle them?**

Subtitle them, in most cases. Synthesising a professional athlete's voice speaking a language they do not speak requires explicit consent, sits inside complex image-rights arrangements that standard contracts rarely cover, and can read as inauthentic to fans who know that player's voice. Dubbing the narration around subtitled player speech is the pattern most organisations settle on.

**How do we handle multilingual squads and accented speech?**

Load a locked vocabulary of every player, staff, club, and competition name before processing, improve capture where you can — lapel and handheld mics, mult-box feeds for press conferences — and always verify the transcript and speaker labels before generating audio. Transcription accuracy on accented, non-native speech is the largest quality variable and is worth testing explicitly during evaluation.

**Can match commentary be dubbed?**

It can be, but it usually should not. Commentary is a performance built on timing, escalation, and local idiom, and translated commentary tends to land flat. Deliver commentary on a separate stem from stadium atmosphere so each market can supply its own commentator or use clean atmosphere.

**What content should a club localize first?**

Evergreen, wholly owned content: player profiles, club history, academy and community features, training-ground material. It carries the fan-attachment value, has a long shelf life so the work is not wasted, and avoids the territorial rights complexity that match footage introduces.

**How do we choose which markets to prioritise?**

Follow demonstrated affinity rather than population — social following by country, merchandise destinations, app usage, and especially the existence of organised fan communities already translating your content informally. Those communities prove demand and often make excellent review partners.
