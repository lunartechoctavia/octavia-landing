## Why Welsh Comes Up

Welsh has around 900,000 speakers, concentrated in Wales, with the Welsh Government's own strategy explicitly targeting growth to a million speakers by 2050 — a genuinely unusual policy goal for a minority language, most of which are managing decline rather than actively planning for growth, and one that has already produced measurable increases in the proportion of school-age children learning Welsh.

For organisations operating in the United Kingdom, Welsh reaches localization lists through a specific and legally grounded route that many companies underestimate the force of. The Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gives Welsh official status in Wales, and the Welsh Language Standards issued under it impose specific, legally enforceable duties on public bodies and on some private organisations providing services in Wales, covering exactly the kind of content — public information, service communication, safety material — that video localization programmes handle.

Beyond the legal driver, there is a genuine and growing Welsh-medium media and education sector, anchored by the dedicated Welsh-language broadcaster S4C, that represents a real audience actively consuming Welsh-language content, not merely a compliance checkbox.

## The Compliance Landscape

**Welsh Language Standards apply directly to specified public bodies operating in Wales**, and compliance is not merely encouraged but subject to a formal regulatory and complaints process through the Welsh Language Commissioner's office, with real consequences for organisations found not to meet their specific duties.

**Private organisations delivering public services or receiving public funding in Wales are frequently subject to standards through their contracts**, even where they are not directly named public bodies themselves, and this contractual mechanism is easy to overlook when assessing whether Welsh obligations apply to a given piece of content, since it depends on the specific funding or contractual relationship rather than only on the organisation's own sector.

**The "no less favourable treatment" principle is central to how these standards are interpreted**, requiring that Welsh-language provision not be treated as a lesser, delayed, or lower-quality version of the English equivalent — a Welsh version published weeks after the English original, or produced to a visibly lower production standard, is a substantive compliance problem under this principle, not merely a customer service shortfall.

**Verify current specific requirements for your sector and organisation type rather than assuming a general Welsh-language policy is sufficient**, since the standards are detailed, sector-specific, and have been subject to ongoing regulatory development — treating this area with the same rigour as any other regulatory compliance question, rather than as a general goodwill gesture, is the correct posture given the actual legal mechanism behind it.

## Consonant Mutation

This is the single grammatical feature most likely to surprise a team without Welsh experience, and it affects text at every level, not just occasional words.

**Welsh initial consonant mutation changes the first sound and letter of a word depending on grammatical context** — the preceding word, the grammatical function, the gender of a preceding noun. A word beginning with "c" can appear as "g," "ngh," or "ch" depending entirely on what triggered the mutation, and all of these are the same underlying word in different mutated forms, not different words or spelling variants.

**This is not optional stylistic variation; using the wrong mutated form, or failing to mutate where required, is a grammatical error**, exactly equivalent to a wrong verb conjugation or a wrong case ending in a more familiar inflected language, and it is precisely the kind of systematic, rule-governed but context-dependent change that automated systems trained on limited Welsh data can get wrong in ways that are immediately obvious to a fluent reader even when every individual word is spelled correctly in isolation.

**Proper nouns, including brand and product names, are subject to mutation when Welsh grammar requires it**, which means a brand name can legitimately appear in a mutated form within otherwise correct Welsh text, and organisations unfamiliar with this can mistake a correct mutation for a corruption of their brand name — deciding and documenting whether your brand name should be allowed to mutate grammatically or should be treated as fixed and exempt is worth doing explicitly rather than leaving to inconsistent per-translator judgement.

## Script, Rendering, and Terminology

**Welsh uses the Latin alphabet with a small number of digraphs treated as single letters for alphabetisation** — ch, dd, ff, ng, ll, ph, rh, th — which affects sorted lists and indexes rendered on screen if a generic Latin collation is applied instead of Welsh-specific collation rules.

**The "ll" sound is a distinctive voiceless lateral fricative with no equivalent in English**, and it is one of the more commonly mispronounced elements in synthetic Welsh speech generation — verify it specifically in any generated audio rather than assuming general text-to-speech quality implies this specific sound is handled correctly, since it is a genuinely unusual sound by cross-linguistic standards and a common failure point.

**Circumflex accents mark long vowels** and are meaningful rather than decorative; do not strip them.

**The Welsh Language Commissioner and related bodies maintain terminology resources for public sector and technical vocabulary**, and using established terms from these resources for regulatory, educational, and public service content is the equivalent of using official EU terminology for other European languages — a specific, correct source to consult rather than generating fresh translations for concepts that already have an established, expected Welsh rendering.

**S4C and the wider Welsh-medium broadcasting sector have their own house conventions for subtitling and dubbing** built over decades of production experience, and aligning with these established conventions, particularly for content likely to reach an audience already familiar with S4C's own output, produces a more credible result than an independently invented style.

## Register, Timing, and Audience

**Welsh has a formal-informal distinction in address**, though usage patterns and the specific contexts where each is expected differ somewhat from the equivalent distinctions in Romance and other European languages — a native Welsh reviewer is the right authority on this for your specific content type rather than assuming direct equivalence with, say, French or German conventions.

**There is meaningful regional variation within Welsh**, broadly between northern and southern dialects, with some vocabulary and pronunciation differences, though the standardised written form used in broadcasting and official contexts is generally intelligible and accepted across both regions — this is a smaller concern than the equivalent regional questions in some other languages covered elsewhere, but worth flagging to a reviewer rather than assuming complete uniformity.

**Bilingual delivery is genuinely normal in Wales in a way that is less common for other minority languages**, with a large share of the Welsh-speaking audience being fully bilingual in English, which means Welsh-language content is very often consumed alongside or in comparison with an English equivalent produced by the same organisation — this makes the "no less favourable treatment" quality bar not just a legal requirement but a genuinely visible and directly comparable one to the actual audience, who can and often will notice a Welsh version that reads as visibly lower effort than its English counterpart.

## A Working Checklist

- Confirm whether your organisation is subject to Welsh Language Standards directly or through a public funding or service contract.
- Treat "no less favourable treatment" as a substantive quality and timeliness bar, not a general aspiration.
- Have a native reviewer check consonant mutation throughout, not just individual word spelling.
- Decide explicitly and document whether your brand name is subject to grammatical mutation or treated as fixed.
- Verify the "ll" sound and other distinctive Welsh phonemes in any generated audio specifically.
- Do not strip circumflex accents marking long vowels.
- Use Welsh Language Commissioner terminology resources for public sector and technical vocabulary.
- Align subtitling and dubbing conventions with established S4C and Welsh-medium broadcasting practice where relevant.
- Confirm formality and address conventions with a native reviewer rather than assuming equivalence to other European languages.
- Flag regional northern-southern vocabulary variation to reviewers without over-indexing on it as a major concern.
- Recognise that your Welsh-speaking audience is very often bilingual and directly comparing Welsh and English versions.
- Publish Welsh and English versions on the same timeline rather than treating Welsh as following at a delay.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Welsh legally required for video content in the UK?**

For specified public bodies operating in Wales, yes, under the Welsh Language Standards issued under the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011, with a formal regulatory complaints process behind it. Private organisations delivering public services or receiving public funding in Wales are also frequently subject to these standards through contractual terms, even without being a public body themselves. Verify your specific obligations rather than assuming Welsh provision is purely discretionary.

**What is consonant mutation and why does it matter for translation quality?**

Welsh systematically changes the first sound and letter of a word depending on grammatical context — the preceding word, grammatical function, or a preceding noun's gender. This is a required grammatical rule, not a spelling variant, and getting it wrong is exactly as noticeable to a fluent reader as a wrong verb conjugation would be in another inflected language. It is also a common weak point for systems with limited Welsh training data, so native review specifically targeting mutation is worth budgeting.

**Will my brand name be changed by Welsh grammar rules?**

It can be, if consonant mutation rules apply to it in a given grammatical context, and this is correct Welsh rather than an error or corruption. Decide explicitly whether you want your brand name to mutate as ordinary Welsh grammar would require, or to be treated as a fixed, exempt form regardless of context, and document that decision so it is applied consistently rather than left to each translator's individual judgement.

**Is Welsh a declining language not worth investing in?**

The opposite, notably among European minority languages. Welsh Government policy explicitly targets growth to a million speakers by 2050, backed by measurable increases in school-age Welsh learning, and it has an active broadcasting and education sector including the dedicated broadcaster S4C. Combined with the legal compliance obligations for many organisations, Welsh is a genuinely growing rather than a purely legacy or symbolic localization target.

**Should Welsh content be published on the same timeline as English content?**

Yes, and doing otherwise is a specific compliance risk, not just a service quality issue. The "no less favourable treatment" principle underlying the Welsh Language Standards is interpreted to mean Welsh provision should not be treated as a delayed or lesser version of the English equivalent. This is also a genuinely visible issue to the audience, since a large share of Welsh speakers are bilingual and can directly compare a delayed or lower-quality Welsh version against its English counterpart.

**Does Welsh vary much between North and South Wales?**

There is real regional variation, mainly in vocabulary and some pronunciation, broadly grouped into northern and southern dialect areas. The standardised written form used in broadcasting, education, and official contexts is generally intelligible and accepted across both regions, so this is a smaller practical obstacle than the equivalent regional questions in some larger languages. It is still worth flagging to a native reviewer rather than assuming complete uniformity, particularly for spoken dubbed content where a strong regional accent choice is more noticeable than it would be in written subtitles.

**Where should I source Welsh technical and public sector terminology?**

The Welsh Language Commissioner and related bodies maintain terminology resources specifically for this purpose, comparable in function to how official EU terminology databases serve other European languages. For content destined for a broadcast-facing or general public audience, aligning with established S4C and Welsh-medium broadcasting conventions is also worth doing, since that sector has built up decades of practical subtitling and dubbing convention that an independently invented house style would not otherwise match.
