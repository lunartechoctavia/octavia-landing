## The Communication Gap Schools Actually Have

Most schools believe they communicate well with families. They send newsletters, post updates, run parent evenings, and maintain a website. From inside the building, the information is plainly available.

From outside, for a family whose home language is not the language of instruction, most of that communication is functionally invisible. The newsletter arrives and is not read. The website update is not seen. The parent evening is attended, or not, by someone who leaves without having understood the part that mattered.

The consequence is not merely that families are less informed. It is that the school's picture of those families is distorted. Non-attendance at a meeting reads as disengagement rather than as a language barrier. A form not returned reads as indifference. Over time, this produces a settled and incorrect belief that certain families are hard to reach, when what is actually true is that nobody has communicated with them in a language they use.

Video is a particularly high-leverage place to fix this, for a reason specific to this audience: it does not require the parent to be literate in their home language. Many adults have strong oral proficiency in a language they read with difficulty, especially where their formal schooling was in another language or was interrupted. A translated letter may not reach them. A translated video will.

## The Obligation Is Usually Stronger Than Schools Assume

In most jurisdictions, schools carry a legal duty to communicate with families in a language they understand, particularly for anything affecting a child's education, placement, discipline, safety, or entitlements.

The duty typically attaches to categories of communication rather than to formats, which means it applies to video just as it applies to letters. Communications commonly covered:

- Enrolment, registration, and eligibility information.
- Special education processes, assessments, and meetings.
- Disciplinary proceedings and their appeal routes.
- Attendance requirements and consequences.
- Health, medication, and safeguarding matters.
- Emergency and safety instructions.
- Anything requiring informed parental consent.

Two points schools frequently get wrong. First, using a bilingual staff member as an ad-hoc interpreter is common and, for consequential matters, often does not satisfy the requirement — many frameworks require qualified interpretation for formal processes. Second, and more seriously, using students to interpret for their own parents is widely prohibited and is always inappropriate for matters concerning that student. It happens constantly, usually because no alternative was available at the moment it was needed.

Building a translated video library reduces both problems. It does not replace qualified interpreters for individual meetings, but it removes a large share of the routine explanatory work that currently falls on whoever happens to be available.

## What to Translate First

Schools produce far more communication than they can localize at once. Priority should follow consequence, not volume.

**First tier — consequence for the child.** Enrolment and registration walkthroughs, special education process explainers, attendance policy, disciplinary process and rights, health and medication procedures, safeguarding information, and anything requiring consent. Misunderstanding here costs a family an entitlement or a child a protection.

**Second tier — participation.** How to use the parent portal, how to check grades, how to contact a teacher, what report cards mean, how parent-teacher conferences work, how to request an interpreter. These determine whether a family can engage at all, and they are permanently useful — recorded once, valuable for years.

**Third tier — transitions.** Starting school, moving between phases, secondary options, exam pathways, post-school routes. High-stakes moments where families with no local experience of the education system are most disadvantaged.

**Fourth tier — routine.** Weekly newsletters, event notices, activity announcements. Highest volume, lowest per-item stakes. Subtitles usually suffice.

The strategic point: the first three tiers are largely evergreen. A district that records and translates them once has a permanent asset, unlike the newsletter treadmill that consumes most communication capacity while reaching the fewest families.

## Register Is the Quality Problem

The most common failure in school translation is not inaccuracy. It is register.

Education systems run on institutional vocabulary that is opaque even to native-speaking parents who did not attend school in that system. Terms describing programme types, support categories, assessment levels, and procedural steps carry precise administrative meanings and no everyday equivalent.

Translating those terms literally produces output that is accurate and useless. The parent now has an unfamiliar term in their own language instead of an unfamiliar term in another one.

What works instead:

**Explain rather than translate the term.** Say what the thing actually is and does before naming it.

**Keep the official name in the source language alongside the explanation.** The parent needs to recognise it on a form, in an email, and on a school sign. Translating the programme name means they cannot find it.

**Avoid acronyms entirely in spoken narration**, or expand them fully on every use. Education acronyms are dense and near-universally opaque to families.

**Write for a parent with no prior experience of this school system.** Not a simplified version for a less capable audience — a version that does not assume institutional knowledge the audience has no way of having acquired.

**Address the parent directly and concretely.** What to do, where, with what, by when. Policy description is far less useful than instruction.

This work has a valuable side effect. Plain-language school communication is better for every family, including the ones who speak the language of instruction fluently. Schools that revise for translation usually find their source communication improves.

## Choosing Languages

Districts should base language selection on their own enrolment data rather than on national or regional statistics.

**Home language survey data at enrolment** is the primary source, and its quality depends entirely on how the survey is administered. Surveys presented as a bureaucratic formality produce underreporting, particularly among families with reasons to be cautious about official questions.

**Interpreter request records** show which languages generate actual demand for support.

**Language of the adult caregiver, not only the student.** A student may be fully proficient in the language of instruction while their parent is not. Communication aimed at parents must follow the parent's language.

**Variety, not just language.** Spanish for families from different regions, Arabic across its varieties, Chinese as Mandarin or Cantonese, and the difference between related languages that administrative systems often collapse. Getting this wrong produces communication that is technically in the right language and practically unusable.

**Emerging communities.** Enrolment can shift quickly with new arrivals. An annual language review is usually too slow; termly is more realistic in districts with mobile populations.

## Student Privacy

School video frequently contains children, and this constrains the workflow in ways general public-sector content does not.

Content showing identifiable students carries privacy obligations that vary by jurisdiction but are consistently strict. Translating and distributing such content does not change those obligations and may extend them, since the audience widens.

Practical requirements:

- Confirm consent covers the intended distribution before localizing content featuring students.
- Treat any content containing individual student records or case detail as confidential, with the same platform controls applied to other sensitive records — access control, encryption, configurable retention, documented data residency, and a written position that content is not used for model training.
- Be cautious about synthesising a child's voice. Consent for filming rarely contemplates voice synthesis, and this warrants a deliberate policy rather than a case-by-case decision.
- Prefer content that explains without featuring identifiable children where the explanation does not require them. Much procedural content can be made with staff and graphics alone, which removes the issue entirely and makes the asset far easier to reuse and update.

## Emergency Communication

School emergencies are the case where language access failure has the most immediate consequence, and where improvisation is most likely.

A lockdown, an early closure, a weather event, a health incident — these generate urgent instructions to families with no time to arrange translation. Districts that handle this well have prepared in advance:

**Pre-translated templates** for the recurring scenarios — closure, delayed start, early dismissal, shelter in place, reunification procedure, health notice — drafted, translated, reviewed, and approved before they are needed, with slots for the variable details.

**Consistent emergency vocabulary** agreed across the district and locked as terminology, so that the same instruction does not appear differently in different notices.

**Multi-format output.** The same message as captioned video, audio for phone systems, and plain text for messaging. Generating audio from a reviewed text version is a fast path to the audio variant.

**A named approver per language** who can authorise release quickly, and a documented acceptance that emergency-tier output carries a different review standard than routine content.

**Post-incident review** of what was actually sent, in which languages, and how quickly. This is the only reliable way to find the gap between the district's language access plan and its practice.

## Working With Interpreters, Not Around Them

Translated video does not remove the need for qualified interpreters, and framing it that way causes justified resistance from staff who understand the difference.

The right framing is workload redistribution. Interpreters currently spend a large share of their time explaining the same routine processes repeatedly — how enrolment works, what the portal does, how conferences are scheduled. That is exactly the content that video handles well.

Moving routine explanation to translated video frees interpreter capacity for the work only a person can do: individual meetings, special education conferences, disciplinary proceedings, safeguarding conversations, and anything requiring genuine two-way dialogue.

Districts that involve their interpreters in producing the video library get better output and better adoption. Interpreters know precisely which explanations families struggle with, which terms cause confusion, and which register works. They are the best available source of requirements, and involving them addresses the reasonable concern that this is a cost-cutting exercise aimed at their roles.

## Sustaining the Programme

School communication programmes frequently start well and fade, because the initial effort is project-funded and the ongoing effort is not resourced.

What determines whether a translated video library survives past its first year:

**Assign ownership to a role, not a person.** Programmes attached to an individual enthusiast end when that person changes job, which in schools is often.

**Keep the evergreen library genuinely evergreen.** Content about enrolment, attendance, special education process, and safeguarding changes rarely. Reviewing it annually is sufficient, and that is a manageable commitment.

**Do not commit to translating routine communication.** Weekly newsletters and event notices are the highest-volume, lowest-value category, and attempting them is what exhausts capacity and kills programmes. Subtitles or a short written summary is enough.

**Build it into existing production.** Where a school already records assemblies, briefings, or open-evening presentations, adding a localization step to that routine costs far less than treating each one as a separate initiative.

**Budget for it explicitly.** A line in the communications or inclusion budget survives leadership changes; an unfunded good intention does not.

**Report on it.** Usage by language, shared with governors or the board once a year, keeps the programme visible and makes the case for its continuation without anyone having to argue for it.

Districts that sustain this find the maintenance burden is far lower than the initial build, provided the scope was set at evergreen content rather than at everything.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a translated video satisfy a school's language access obligation?**

For general and informational communication, it can, and it is often more effective than translated text because many parents have stronger oral than written proficiency in their home language. For individualised, consequential proceedings — special education meetings, disciplinary hearings, safeguarding conversations — qualified interpretation is typically required and video does not substitute. Check the standard applicable in your jurisdiction and content category.

**Can we use bilingual staff or students to interpret?**

Bilingual staff may be appropriate for informal communication but frequently do not meet the qualification standard for formal processes. Using students to interpret for their own families is widely prohibited and always inappropriate for matters concerning that student. Building a translated video library substantially reduces the situations where this happens out of necessity.

**How do we handle school-specific terminology?**

Explain what the thing is in plain language, and keep the official name in the source language so parents can recognise it on forms, emails, and signage. Translating programme and category names leaves families unable to locate the thing you described. Lock these as terminology so they are handled consistently across every video.

**What about videos featuring students?**

Confirm that existing consent covers the intended distribution before localizing, treat the content with the same privacy controls as other student records, and adopt a deliberate policy on synthesising children's voices rather than deciding case by case. Where possible, produce procedural content using staff and graphics only, which avoids the issue and makes the asset easier to maintain.

**Which languages should a district support?**

Use your own home language survey and interpreter request data rather than regional statistics, follow the caregiver's language rather than the student's, and be specific about variety rather than treating a language as monolithic. Review termly rather than annually where enrolment is mobile.
