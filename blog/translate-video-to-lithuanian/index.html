Translate Video to Lithuanian: Seven Cases, Declined Names, and EU Compliance

Lithuanian will grammatically inflect your product name whether you planned for it or not.

Why Lithuanian Comes Up

Lithuanian has roughly three million speakers, most in Lithuania, with significant communities in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Norway, and the United States following substantial post-accession emigration.

It reaches localization lists for three reasons. Lithuania is an EU member state and Lithuanian is an official EU language, bringing public sector and regulatory obligations. Lithuania has developed a notably large financial technology and business services sector relative to its size, which generates internal and product content. And it is one of three Baltic markets that companies typically address together, alongside Latvian and Estonian.

That last point produces a persistent misconception worth clearing up immediately. The three Baltic languages are not related in the way the grouping implies. Lithuanian and Latvian are Baltic languages and are related to each other, though not mutually intelligible. Estonian is Finno-Ugric and is related to Finnish, not to either of the others. Nothing about a Lithuanian translation transfers to Estonian, and treating the three as one linguistic region produces bad estimates.

What Makes Lithuanian Distinctive

Lithuanian is of considerable interest to historical linguists because it retains features of Proto-Indo-European that most European languages lost. For localization purposes this translates into one practical fact: it is heavily inflected, more so than most languages a pipeline will have been tuned on.

Seven cases — nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, instrumental, locative, and vocative — applied across two grammatical genders and two numbers, with several declension classes. Every noun, adjective, pronoun, numeral, and participle inflects.

No articles, definite or indefinite. Definiteness is conveyed through word order, demonstratives, and a distinction between simple and pronominal adjective forms.

An unusually rich participle system. Lithuanian has more participial forms than most European languages, including forms that mark evidentiality — whether the speaker witnessed something or is reporting it. Translation from English, which has nothing equivalent, requires a choice that the source does not supply.

Pitch accent. Lithuanian has both stress placement and, on stressed syllables, a distinction between rising and falling tone. Stress placement is mobile and shifts within a word's paradigm. This matters for generated audio, since incorrect stress is immediately audible.

Dual number survives in restricted use, alongside singular and plural.

The combined effect is that Lithuanian output requires more review than most European languages of comparable market size, and that review should be budgeted rather than discovered.

Declining Foreign Names

This is the feature that most often surprises teams and most often produces an escalation, so it is worth understanding before it happens.

Lithuanian grammatical convention adapts and declines foreign proper nouns rather than leaving them in their original form. A foreign name appearing in running text takes Lithuanian case endings, and it is frequently also respelled to reflect Lithuanian orthographic conventions.

The consequence for a brand is that its name will not appear in the nominative form throughout a Lithuanian text. It will appear with a range of endings depending on its syntactic role, and possibly with adapted spelling.

Reactions to this vary. Some organisations treat it as unacceptable and insist the name appear unmodified. Some accept it as correct target-language behaviour. Both positions are defensible and the decision needs to be made explicitly, because the default — leaving it to the translator — produces inconsistency across a catalogue.

If you require the unmodified form, say so in the style guide and expect the text around it to be constructed to accommodate an indeclinable name, which occasionally produces stilted phrasing. If you accept declension, lock the paradigm — the specific set of inflected forms — so the same forms are used everywhere rather than being re-derived per asset.

The same applies to product names, feature names, and the names of people appearing in content.

Script and Rendering

Lithuanian uses the Latin alphabet with ą, č, ę, ė, į, š, ų, ū, and ž.

Font coverage needs verification. The ogonek characters — ą, ę, į, ų — are the ones most often missing or badly rendered, and ė and ū are also worth checking. A font that handles Polish will usually handle the ogoneks but may miss ė.

Do not strip diacritics. They are phonemic. Lithuanian written without them is comprehensible in informal messaging and unacceptable in published content, and it introduces genuine ambiguity because several diacritic and non-diacritic pairs are distinct words.

Encoding. UTF-8 throughout. Legacy Baltic encodings appear in older material.

Collation. Lithuanian alphabetical order places the diacritic letters in specific positions rather than treating them as variants of the base letter, which affects sorted lists rendered on screen.

Text expansion runs roughly fifteen to twenty percent longer than English — higher than the Slavic averages, driven by the case endings and the fuller morphology. This is enough to break subtitle layouts fitted to English and enough that dubbed narration runs meaningfully longer.

Grammar That Breaks Automated Translation

Case selection under ambiguity. Seven cases must be assigned from syntactic roles English marks weakly or not at all. Prepositions govern specific cases, and the same preposition can govern different cases with different meanings.

Adjective and participle agreement across case, number, and gender, propagating through long noun phrases. A single wrong case on the head noun cascades through everything agreeing with it.

Verbal aspect and the participle system. Choosing between participial constructions where English uses a subordinate clause is a judgement the source does not determine.

Reflexive verb forms carry a range of meanings including passive and reciprocal senses, and the mapping from English constructions is not one to one.

Numeral government. The case and number of a noun following a numeral depends on the numeral's value, with different rules for different ranges. Any content containing figures is exposed to this, which is most instructional and commercial content.

Mobile stress. Stress position shifts within a word's inflectional paradigm according to accent class. This is a generation problem rather than a translation one, but it is the most audible error in synthesised Lithuanian.

Register and Address

Lithuanian distinguishes formal and informal second person, and the formal is used more broadly than in Nordic or Anglophone convention.

Formal address is expected in institutional, governmental, financial, medical, legal, and business-to-business communication, and also in much consumer-facing commercial communication where English or Scandinavian brands would use informal.

Informal address suits creator content, entertainment, advertising to younger audiences, and consumer applications.

Mixing within one asset is an error.

Lithuanian public communication tends toward a fuller, more explicit register than contemporary English marketing copy. Terse English lines translated literally read as abrupt or incomplete.

Timing and Dubbing

Lithuanian default order is subject-verb-object, though it is flexible because case marks role.

  • Fifteen to twenty percent text expansion means narration runs noticeably longer than the source. This is the single most consequential planning fact for Lithuanian dubbing.
  • Case endings attach to word ends; verify they survive subtitle segment boundaries intact.
  • Confirm stress placement in generated audio, particularly on inflected forms where stress has shifted from the base word.
  • Verify long vowels and the ogonek vowels are rendered correctly in audio; they are contrastive.
  • Confirm speaker separation before generating multi-speaker audio.

Lithuania subtitles most adult content and dubs children's programming, with voice-over translation also present in some broadcast contexts.

Terminology and EU Context

EU institutional and regulatory vocabulary is officially standardised in Lithuanian. Regulations, directives, institutional names, and legal concepts have official Lithuanian renderings published by the EU, and content touching regulatory, compliance, consumer rights, or financial topics should use them.

Lithuanian has an active language regulation body — the State Commission of the Lithuanian Language — which issues normative guidance on terminology and usage. Its rulings carry weight in public-sector and published contexts, and terminology decisions in formal registers should follow them.

There is a preference for native coinages over international borrowings in formal and institutional register, though technology and software vocabulary borrows more freely in professional practice. As in Croatian, applying the wrong end of this spectrum to the wrong register reads as foreign.

Financial technology terminology is worth attention given the sector's presence, and established Lithuanian forms exist for concepts that a general translator would render differently.

Numbers and dates. Comma as decimal separator, space as thousands separator, year-month-day order is standard in official use with day-month-year also common. Verify conversion.

A Working Checklist

  • Do not treat the three Baltic languages as one linguistic group; Estonian is unrelated to Lithuanian and Latvian.
  • Decide explicitly whether brand and product names may be declined, and lock the paradigm if they may.
  • Verify ą, ę, į, ų, ė, and ū render correctly and are never stripped.
  • Confirm UTF-8 encoding end to end.
  • Budget fifteen to twenty percent text expansion in layouts and dub timing.
  • Have a native reviewer check case selection, participle choice, and numeral government.
  • Verify stress placement in generated audio on inflected forms.
  • Decide formal or informal address once, and expect formal to be appropriate more often than in Nordic markets.
  • Use official EU Lithuanian terminology for regulatory and legal concepts.
  • Follow State Commission guidance for formal and public-sector register.
  • Apply native coinages in institutional register and accept borrowings in technical register.
  • Provide Lithuanian captions alongside Lithuanian audio.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Lithuanian, Latvian, and Estonian related?

Only partly. Lithuanian and Latvian are Baltic languages related to each other, though not mutually intelligible — a Lithuanian speaker does not understand Latvian without study. Estonian is Finno-Ugric and related to Finnish, with no relationship to either. Grouping the three as "the Baltics" is a geographic and commercial convention, not a linguistic one, and nothing transfers between Lithuanian and Estonian.

Will Lithuanian change my brand name?

By default, yes. Lithuanian convention adapts and declines foreign proper nouns, so your name will appear with case endings and possibly with adapted spelling throughout the text. You can require the unmodified form, and you should decide which you want explicitly — the default of leaving it to each translator produces inconsistency across a catalogue. If you accept declension, lock the specific inflected forms.

How much longer is Lithuanian than English?

Roughly fifteen to twenty percent in character count, which is higher than the Slavic languages and driven by the case endings and richer morphology. This breaks subtitle layouts fitted tightly to English and makes dubbed narration meaningfully longer than the source. It is the single most important planning number for a Lithuanian project.

What causes the most translation errors?

Case selection and numeral government. Seven cases across multiple declension classes must be assigned from syntactic roles that English marks weakly, and prepositions govern specific cases with meaning changes. Separately, the case and number of a noun after a numeral depends on the numeral's value, which exposes any content containing figures — most instructional and commercial material.

Why does synthesised Lithuanian sometimes sound wrong even when the text is correct?

Almost always stress placement. Lithuanian stress is mobile and shifts position within a word's inflectional paradigm according to accent class, so the stress on an inflected form is frequently not where it falls on the base word. Lithuanian also distinguishes rising and falling tone on stressed syllables. Both are immediately audible to native listeners and invisible in the text.

Is Lithuanian mutually intelligible with Latvian?

No, despite both being the only two surviving Baltic languages and sharing a common ancestor. A Lithuanian speaker cannot follow Latvian speech or text without study, and vice versa — the two diverged enough that they function as fully separate localization targets requiring their own translation, terminology, and voice work, with essentially no shared assets beyond the general observation that both retain unusually conservative Indo-European grammatical features relative to most of their European neighbours.

Should I use formal or informal address?

Formal more often than you would in a Nordic or Anglophone market. It is expected in institutional, governmental, financial, medical, and legal content, and also in much consumer-facing commercial communication where an English brand would default to informal. Reserve informal for creator content, entertainment, and products aimed at younger audiences.
