Translate Video into Korean: Honorifics, Timing, and Market Fit

Korean encodes social relationships directly into its grammar, which makes register the defining decision in Korean localization. This guide covers speech levels, timing, and the conventions that determine whether content reads as native.

Why Korea Is Worth the Effort

South Korea has one of the world's highest rates of internet penetration, mobile video consumption, and online spending per capita. It is a mature market for online education, software, gaming, beauty, and entertainment, and Korean audiences are demanding consumers of digital content with high expectations for production quality.

Korean is spoken by roughly 80 million people, concentrated in South Korea and North Korea with substantial diaspora communities in China, the United States, Japan, and Central Asia. For most commercial content programs, South Korea is the operative market.

Korean also carries outsized cultural influence relative to its speaker count. Korean entertainment, beauty, and food content has global reach, and Korean-language versions of international content often perform well beyond Korea itself among learners and diaspora audiences.

Speech Levels: The Defining Decision

Korean grammar encodes the relationship between speaker and listener directly into verb endings. This is not an optional stylistic layer — every sentence must select a speech level, and there is no neutral option that avoids the choice.

Korean has several speech levels, of which three matter most for video content:

Formal polite (합쇼체) is the highest register in general use, marked by verb endings in -습니다 / -ㅂ니다. It is the register of news broadcasting, formal presentations, corporate announcements, public address, and customer service. It signals respect and professional distance. For corporate video, executive communications, formal training, and anything addressing customers in a business context, this is the correct default.

Polite informal (해요체) uses -어요 / -아요 endings. It is polite but warmer and less distant — the register of everyday courteous conversation, most consumer-facing content, tutorials, and creator video. It is the most common register in Korean digital content aimed at general audiences. For instructional content, product explainers, and lifestyle material, this often fits better than the formal polite level, which can read as stiff.

Casual (반말) drops honorific endings entirely. It is used among close friends, with children, and by speakers addressing someone clearly junior. In content, it appears in entertainment, some youth-oriented material, and dialogue between characters. Using it to address a general audience is inappropriate and will read as rude rather than friendly.

The choice is not merely stylistic. Addressing a professional audience in casual speech is a social error, not a tone preference. Conversely, using formal polite speech throughout a casual creator video produces something that sounds like a corporate announcement.

Consistency is mandatory. Because the speech level is carried in every verb ending, drift between levels within a video is immediately audible and reads as a translation error rather than a stylistic choice. Decide the level before translation begins, record it in the project brief, and have the reviewer check specifically for consistency.

Honorifics Beyond Speech Levels

Separate from speech level, Korean has a system of subject honorifics that mark respect toward the person being discussed, not the person being addressed. The honorific infix -시- and a set of suppletive honorific vocabulary items (different words for "eat," "sleep," "be," and others when the subject is respected) operate independently of the ending-level choice.

This matters in content that discusses people. Referring to a customer, a senior figure, or a respected third party without appropriate honorifics reads as dismissive. Referring to oneself or one's own company with honorifics reads as arrogant.

Title usage follows related conventions. Korean professional address typically uses job titles rather than personal names — a manager is addressed and referred to by title, often with an honorific suffix. Direct translation of English name usage into Korean produces text that reads as inappropriately familiar.

These are reviewer-catchable issues rather than things a project owner can verify without Korean fluency, which is why native review is non-negotiable for Korean content addressing or discussing people.

Text Contraction and Timing

Korean generally contracts relative to English rather than expanding. Translated Korean text is often 10 to 20 percent shorter in duration than the English source, though this varies by content type and register — formal polite endings are longer than casual ones, which narrows the gap for formal content.

Contraction is easier to manage than expansion but is not free. Segments that come in noticeably short leave gaps where the speaker is visibly still talking, or produce audio that finishes early and sits in silence. For dubbed content, this reads as loose rather than rushed, which is less objectionable but still noticeable.

The response is to use the recovered time productively rather than leaving it as dead air: allow slightly more natural pacing, add appropriate pauses at clause boundaries, and let the delivery breathe. Korean prosody accommodates deliberate pacing well.

For subtitles, contraction is an advantage. Korean subtitle text fits comfortably in the available time in most cases, which permits slightly longer display durations and improves comprehension.

Subtitle Conventions

Korean subtitle practice has its own norms, and Korean audiences are experienced subtitle readers.

Line length conventions run to roughly 16 to 20 Korean characters per line, with a strong preference for one or two lines. Korean characters are syllable blocks, each carrying substantially more information than a Latin letter, so character counts are much lower than English equivalents for the same content.

Reading speed for Korean is typically measured in characters per second, with guidance around 6 to 9 characters per second for general audiences. Because each syllable block is information-dense, per-character reading time is higher than for Latin scripts.

Korean text has spaces between words (unlike Chinese and Japanese), which makes line breaking more straightforward. Breaks should still fall at phrase boundaries rather than at arbitrary character counts, and particles should stay attached to the words they modify.

Korean uses standard punctuation forms broadly compatible with Latin conventions, though full-width variants appear in some contexts. Mixed Latin text — product names, technical terms — is common and normal in Korean subtitles.

Font coverage should be verified for burned-in subtitles. Hangul requires complete syllable block coverage, and fonts with partial coverage will fail on less common blocks.

Loanwords and Terminology

Korean borrows extensively from English, particularly in technology, business, beauty, and lifestyle domains. Borrowed terms are written in Hangul phonetic adaptation rather than Latin script in most contexts.

This creates a three-way choice for any given technical term: use the established Korean-adapted loanword, use a native Korean or Sino-Korean equivalent, or retain the Latin-script English term. All three appear in real Korean content, and the correct choice varies by term and context.

Established loanwords should be used in their established form. Inventing a new phonetic adaptation for a term that already has a conventional Korean rendering produces text that looks wrong even though the phonetics are defensible. This is a case where a glossary validated by a native reviewer prevents a large number of small errors.

Sino-Korean vocabulary — words derived from Chinese roots — carries a more formal register than native Korean equivalents, similar to the Latinate-versus-Germanic register distinction in English. Technical and academic content leans Sino-Korean; conversational content leans native Korean. Register consistency applies here as well as in speech levels.

Product names and trademarks generally stay in Latin script. Confirm against any existing Korean-language interface you ship, since the video must match what users see in the product.

Format Preference: Subtitles or Dubbing

Korea has a strong subtitle tradition for foreign entertainment content. Films and dramas are typically subtitled rather than dubbed for adult audiences, with dubbing reserved primarily for children's programming and some animation.

This shapes expectations for entertainment content but does not govern all categories. For instructional, educational, and corporate content, dubbed Korean audio performs well because it removes the attention split between watching a demonstration and reading text. Korean audiences do not reject dubbed instructional content the way they might reject a dubbed feature film.

The practical guidance: subtitle entertainment and personality-driven content where the original voice carries value, dub instructional and demonstration content where visual attention is required, and offer both for high-value assets.

Voice Selection

Korean voice selection should match both the speech level and the content register. A voice with a formal broadcast delivery paired with polite informal endings produces a mismatch between what is said and how it sounds.

Match apparent age and gender to the source speaker where the speaker is visible. Korean audiences are attentive to voice-persona fit, and mismatches read as jarring in on-camera content.

Delivery pacing should account for contraction. Because Korean text tends to come in shorter than the English source, a voice with measured pacing fills the available time more naturally than one with rapid delivery, which would leave larger gaps.

For content built around a specific recognizable presenter, voice cloning carries the speaker's identity into Korean, keeping the on-screen person and the voice consistent rather than substituting an unrelated narrator.

Numbers, Dates, and Formats

Korean uses two numeral systems in parallel, and choosing between them is not optional — each is required in specific contexts.

Sino-Korean numerals are used for dates, money, phone numbers, measurements, minutes, and most counting above small quantities. Native Korean numerals are used for hours, ages, and counting with certain counter words, generally up to ninety-nine. A generated voice that reads an hour using Sino-Korean numerals, or a price using native Korean numerals, produces text that is comprehensible but immediately marks the audio as non-native.

Counter words compound this. Korean requires a classifier between a numeral and a noun, and the correct classifier depends on what is being counted — different counters for people, flat objects, bound volumes, machines, animals, and so on. Translated content that uses a generic counter throughout reads as simplified rather than natural.

Dates are written year-month-day, the reverse of common English ordering. Currency is the won, written with the ₩ symbol or the Korean word, and Korean pricing conventions frequently express large figures in units of ten thousand (만), which does not map cleanly onto English thousand-based grouping. A price stated as "fifty thousand won" in English is naturally expressed as "5만 원" in Korean.

Measurements are metric. Korean also retains some traditional units in specific domains, particularly real estate, but these rarely appear in general content.

Quality Review for Korean

The review brief for Korean should lead with register, because speech level errors outrank almost everything else in perceived severity.

Ask the reviewer to confirm that a single speech level is maintained from the first sentence to the last. Ask them to check subject honorifics wherever a person is mentioned — customers, executives, third parties — since these operate independently of the speech level and are a separate source of error.

Ask for a terminology pass against the glossary, with specific attention to loanword forms. An established Korean rendering of an English term is not something a translator can derive; it either matches conventional usage or it does not, and only a native reviewer will know.

For dubbed audio, have the reviewer listen to the full track. Numeral system errors, counter word errors, and awkward prosody are audible issues that a script read will not reliably surface. Korean intonation carries sentence-type information, and a statement delivered with question intonation changes how a listener parses it.

Ask the reviewer to rate severity. Korean has a wide range of defensible stylistic choices, and a reviewer who flags everything at equal weight makes triage impossible. Distinguishing blocking errors from preferences keeps the revision cycle short.

Practical Sequence

Confirm the target market — South Korea in nearly all commercial cases — and the audience relationship, which determines speech level.

Choose the speech level explicitly and record it. Formal polite for corporate, customer-facing, and formal training content. Polite informal for tutorials, creator content, and general consumer material. Casual only for entertainment contexts where it is clearly appropriate.

Build a terminology glossary covering loanword forms, Sino-Korean versus native vocabulary decisions, and product name handling. Have it validated by a Korean reviewer before applying it at scale.

Translate, then have a native speaker review with a specific brief: speech level consistency, honorific correctness where people are discussed, loanword forms, and anything that reads as translated rather than written.

Select a voice matching the register, test a sample, generate, and check timing — paying attention to segments that finish early rather than only to overruns.

Verify subtitle rendering, including font coverage and line breaking, on both desktop and mobile.

Korean rewards precision. The grammatical encoding of social relationship means that errors which would be minor tone issues in other languages become clear social missteps in Korean — and conversely, getting register right produces content that Korean audiences recognize as made for them rather than adapted at them.
