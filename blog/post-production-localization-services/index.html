Adding Localization to Your Post-Production Service Menu

Your client already paid for the edit. Selling them nine more versions of it is the easiest upsell in post.

The Upsell That Is Already Sitting in Your Project Folder

Post houses and production companies spend enormous effort winning projects and comparatively little extracting full value from the ones they have already delivered.

Consider the shape of a typical corporate or brand engagement. You scoped it, shot it, edited it, graded it, mixed it, and delivered a master. The client is happy. The project closes. Six weeks later, someone in the client's German office asks whether there is a German version, and the answer is that there could be, for roughly the cost of a new production, so there isn't one.

That conversation happens constantly and it represents revenue leaving the building. The client wants more versions of an asset they have already invested in. The obstacle has always been that producing them required re-recording voice talent, re-conforming timelines, and re-mixing — which made the ninth language cost nearly as much as the first.

That constraint has changed. Which means localization is now a service line a post house can offer at a margin, using assets it already holds, to clients it has already sold to.

The firms doing this well are not positioning it as a discount alternative to traditional dubbing. They are positioning it as a natural extension of delivery — the same way a colourist offers HDR versions or a mix engineer offers stem deliverables.

What You Are Actually Selling

Being precise about the deliverable prevents both underquoting and client disappointment.

Subtitle packages. Timed, translated subtitle files in the client's required formats, optionally with burned-in styled versions for social. The simplest product, the fastest turnaround, and the easiest entry point.

Voiced versions. Translated narration replacing the original voiceover, laid against the existing music and effects bed, delivered as a full mix and optionally as stems.

Full localized masters. Voiced audio plus localized on-screen graphics, lower thirds, titles, and end cards. This is where post houses have a genuine structural advantage, because the graphics work requires access to project files and craft skills the client does not have.

Accessibility deliverables. Same-language captions and transcripts, which are a distinct requirement from translated subtitles and are increasingly mandated for corporate and public-sector clients.

Archive conversion. Bulk processing of a client's existing library. Lower per-asset value, high volume, and often the largest single engagement available.

The full localized master is the highest-value product and the one clients cannot self-serve. A client can run a video through a translation tool themselves. They cannot rebuild the motion graphics package in Japanese with correct typography and reflowed layouts. That is the defensible part of the offering and it should anchor the positioning.

Preparing Projects for Localization Before You Need To

The most profitable localization work is done on projects that were built to be localized. This costs almost nothing at edit time and saves a great deal later.

Keep a clean M&E. A mix delivered with music and effects separate from dialogue makes voiced versions straightforward. Where the project only exists as a full mix, dialogue separation can recover a usable bed, but starting with proper stems is always better.

Keep text as text. Titles, lower thirds, and captions built as editable text layers rather than flattened graphics or baked-in renders. This single practice determines whether a localized graphics package takes an hour or a day.

Leave room in the layout. German and Finnish run substantially longer than English; Chinese and Japanese run shorter but need different line-breaking and vertical space. Lower-third boxes designed to exactly fit the English string will break in half your target languages. Designing with thirty percent expansion headroom costs nothing at design time.

Avoid burning text into picture. On-screen text composited into the master forces a re-render for every language and, where the plate is unavailable, may make localization impossible.

Retain a textless version. The single most valuable archival artefact for future localization work.

Keep project files organised and archived. Obvious, routinely neglected, and the difference between a profitable localization job and an unprofitable reconstruction exercise.

Production companies that adopt these as standard practice can quote localization confidently on any past project. Those that do not will find that half their back catalogue is effectively unlocalizable.

Fitting It Into the Workflow

The practical integration question is where localization sits relative to picture lock.

The answer is firmly after. Localizing before picture lock means redoing it, and clients who ask for parallel workstreams to save time are almost always creating rework. The correct sequence:

Picture lock, then final mix with stems retained, then transcript generation and cleanup, then client approval of the source transcript, then translation with terminology applied, then client-side or reviewer-side approval of the translation, then voice generation, then graphics localization, then mix, then delivery.

Two of those steps deserve emphasis because they are the ones inexperienced teams skip.

Source transcript approval. Getting the client to sign off on the source transcript before anything is translated is the highest-value process control available. It catches the "actually we should change that line" conversation once, in the source, rather than nine times across nine languages after audio has been generated. It costs an email and saves entire rounds.

Translation approval before generation. Reviewing and correcting text is fast and cheap. Regenerating audio after the fact is neither. Any workflow that generates voice before the translation has been reviewed is manufacturing rework.

Pricing the Service

Post houses tend to price by time, which fits badly here because the time varies enormously by asset while the client's perception of value does not.

More workable models:

Per finished minute, per language, by tier. Subtitle tier, voiced tier, full-master tier. Clients understand this immediately because it maps to how they already buy captioning and dubbing.

Setup plus per-language. A one-time preparation charge covering transcript cleanup, terminology build, and graphics templating, then a lower per-language rate. This prices the reality that the first language carries most of the work and the ninth carries very little, and it makes adding languages feel cheap — which encourages clients to add them.

Retained library rate. For clients with continuous output, a monthly rate covering a defined volume. Predictable for both sides and it makes localization a default rather than a per-project decision.

The setup-plus-per-language model is usually the most commercially effective, because the marginal cost of an additional language is genuinely low and pricing that transparently converts a two-language client into a six-language client.

On margin: it comes from the preparation and graphics work, not from marking up processing. Be relaxed about the processing line item being visible and competitive. Charge properly for transcript preparation, terminology, graphics localization, mix, and QC, which is where the craft is.

Quality Control You Can Actually Perform

The uncomfortable question for a post house: how do you QC a language nobody in the building speaks?

You do not need to assess translation quality to catch most defects. A technical QC pass by a competent operator catches the majority of real problems:

  • Audio sync against picture at start, middle, and end of every asset.
  • Loudness and true-peak compliance to the client's delivery standard.
  • Dialogue level relative to the music and effects bed.
  • No clipped, truncated, or missing segments.
  • Every speaker present and distinct where multiple speakers exist.
  • Subtitle timing, reading speed, line length, and no overlap with on-screen graphics.
  • Graphics text fitting its container without clipping or overflow at every safe-area boundary.
  • Correct rendering of accented and non-Latin characters throughout, including in file names and metadata.
  • Frame rate and timecode consistency across the deliverable set.

For linguistic quality, build a small reviewer network — one trusted native speaker per language you offer regularly — and price their pass into the tier. Do not attempt to assess it internally and do not quietly skip it. Clients discover unreviewed output eventually, and the reputational cost exceeds what the review would have cost.

Educating the Client

Most clients have either no mental model of localization cost or one calibrated to traditional dubbing rates from a decade ago. Both produce difficult conversations.

Three points, delivered early, prevent most of them.

Set expectations about what synthetic voice is and is not. It is clear, consistent, natural-sounding, and appropriate for narration, training, and explanatory content. It is not a performance. For a brand film whose emotional impact rests on vocal delivery, say so and quote a human option. Clients respect the distinction and remember who was straight with them.

Explain the source-quality dependency. Output quality is bounded by input quality. Overlapping dialogue, heavy background noise, strong dialect variation, and unscripted rambling all degrade results. Clients who understand this before delivery interpret an imperfect result as a source problem rather than a vendor failure.

Show a sample before scoping. Run thirty seconds of the client's own content through the workflow in one target language and show it. It resolves more uncertainty in two minutes than any amount of explanation, and it converts.

Getting Started

Pick three past projects where you still hold clean stems, textless plates, and organised project files. Produce a localized version of each in one language, at your own cost, as portfolio material.

Take those to the clients they belong to. The conversation is not a pitch; it is showing someone a finished version of something they already own in a market they already operate in.

Use whatever that surfaces to build your rate card, your delivery specification template, and your reviewer network. Then make the localization conversation a standard part of every new project scoping call, and make textless deliverables and editable text layers standard practice on every job regardless of whether localization is currently in scope.

The compounding effect is the point. Every project delivered localization-ready is a future upsell that costs you nothing to preserve.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do we need to be able to speak the target languages to offer this?

No, but you need access to people who can. Technical QC — sync, levels, timing, character rendering, graphics fit — is language-independent and catches most defects. Linguistic review requires a native speaker, and the right structure is a small network of trusted reviewers priced into your higher tiers rather than an attempt to assess quality internally.

What if the client's original project has no separated audio stems?

Automated dialogue separation can recover a usable music and effects bed from a full mix. It is not equivalent to a properly delivered stem under critical listening, but for most corporate, training, and explanatory content it is entirely serviceable. For premium brand work where the mix is scrutinised, flag the limitation during scoping.

How should we price the first language versus additional ones?

Separate setup from per-language delivery. The first language carries transcript preparation, terminology, and graphics templating; subsequent languages carry very little of that. Pricing it transparently makes adding languages feel inexpensive to the client, which is exactly the behaviour you want to encourage.

Should we tell clients the voices are synthetic?

Yes, explicitly and in the statement of work. It is a defining characteristic of the deliverable, clients in regulated sectors may have disclosure obligations of their own, and discovering it later damages trust disproportionately. Framing your tiers around the degree of human involvement makes it a feature of the offer rather than an awkward disclosure.

What is the single most valuable habit to adopt now?

Keep text as editable text layers and always archive a textless version. Those two practices determine whether your back catalogue is localizable at a profit or requires reconstruction, and they cost essentially nothing to adopt at edit time.
